# Cybele Blog Cover - the brief

> **A cover is designed by a person.** This file says what the brief that precedes it must contain,
> and deliberately says nothing about how a cover looks: composition, treatment, palette and craft
> are the designer's decisions, and a suggestion there is noise she has to argue with.
>
> The process - when the brief is written, its shape, where the file goes - is `SKILL.md` Phase 2.
> What belongs in it is here.
>
> The generative system this file used to be - archetypes, gradient recipes, hero and satellite
> vocabulary, SVG construction - is **retired**. It lived at the end of this file and was read on
> every run without governing anything; it now lives in `archive/`, which nothing loads.

---

## 0. The brief is written in Spanish

**Every line of `COVER_BRIEF.md` is in Spanish - the whole thing, not only the labels.** This file
and the rest of the skill are in English because they are the rules; the brief is not a rule, it is
a piece of work handed to the designer, who reads and thinks in Spanish. A brief in English costs her
a translation on every line, which is the opposite of what the brief is for.

- The **article is in English**, so the brief is a reading of it, never a transcription: say the idea
  in Spanish rather than quoting the sentence.
- The **article title** at the top keeps the article's own words.
- **This does not extend to the illustrations.** Their titles, card titles and descriptions are the
  text that gets published, so they stay in the article's language. The brief is internal; the
  illustration is not.

## 1. The title is the frame

**The cover is published beside the title, and they are read as one thing.** That is the whole reason
this brief exists: everything in it is here so a designer can draw something that stands next to
*that* title and completes it. So the title is read first, and every line is written against it.

**The test that catches the common failure:** could this brief sit in front of a different article
with the same mechanism? If the words would work just as well for a bank, a warehouse or a call
centre, the article was not read — the product was.

**When the title names who or where, that outranks how.** "for Healthcare", "in retail", "for
banking" is the subject of the article; smart cards, gateways and licences are the mechanism. A cover
of the mechanism alone could accompany any article about badges, and beside a title that says
*Healthcare* it looks like it belongs to something else.

> A real case, and the reason this section exists. For *"Smart Card Redirection for VDI: Tap-and-Go
> Fingerprint Authentication for Healthcare on OCI"* the brief returned **carnet, huella, lector,
> puesto compartido**: four pieces of mechanism, no person, no place, nothing clinical. The article
> opens on a hospital floor, with a nurse roaming between exam rooms and med carts — it was in the
> first paragraph and in the title, and the brief dropped both.
>
> And the correction overshot, which is the other half of the lesson. The replacement list named the
> clinician, the record, the ward and the med cart: four words about healthcare and nothing about the
> fingerprint, the access or the virtual desktop, which is what the article is for. Adding the who
> does not mean spending the whole list on it. The set has to **cover** the article, and *Palabras
> clave* below says what that means.

## 2. What a cover is about

- **It shows what is at stake, never the product.** The problem, or what changes once it is solved -
  both are fair game. What is never on a cover is the product itself: no interface, no logo, no
  architecture diagram, no Thinfinity. The article argues; the cover shows what the argument is
  about.
- **One or two elements.** Never an element plus an element plus an arrow.
- **A category is not a drawable noun.** "VDI", "cloud", "remote access" name a market, not a thing.
  What is drawable is who is sitting there: the teller, the nurse, the branch, the counter.
- **It does not explain the article.** It represents the title and the problem, and stops.
- **The brief does not propose the image.** It says what the article is, in three lines. Finding what
  to draw is the designer's work - the five rhetorical devices that used to generate scenes here were
  retired because they read well and did not help her do it.

## 3. What each line of the brief has to do

The brief is three lines. It used to carry an *Idea*, five rhetorical devices with three scenes
each, a *Representar* and a *Naturaleza*; those are retired — the scenes read well and did not help
the designer work, which is the only test that counts.

### Sin tecnicismos
**The whole article, retold to someone who knows nothing about technology.** Three or four sentences:
what the situation is, what the article proposes, and why that would matter to a person.

- **Not one piece of jargon, not one acronym, not one product category.** Where a concept needs one,
  say what the thing *is for a person* instead: not "virtual desktops", but the screen someone opens
  to start working; not "the cloud", but companies that rent out computers over the internet. Words
  everyone already uses are fine — the test is not a banned list, it is whether someone who has never
  heard the word "cloud" follows the paragraph without stopping.
- **It is an explanation, not a scene.** It says what the article is about, in plain words. It does
  not reach for "it is like a warehouse with three sheds": the images are the designer's to find.
- **It covers the article, not only the pain.** *Problema* handles the pain. This line says what the
  whole piece is about, including what it proposes.
- **It invents nothing.** Everything in it is in the article.
- **What the article proposes has to differ from what is already true.** Read the proposal sentence
  back against the situation sentence: if it would also be an accurate description of today, it says
  nothing. "It proposes that the screen could be in any of the three" fails — it is already in one of
  the three. Plain words make this kind of emptiness visible; jargon hides it, which is half the
  reason this line exists.

This line is also the honesty check on the one below it. A problem understood only in the article's
own vocabulary is a problem not understood: a brief that read "you cannot get in" when the article
said "you get in three times" would have failed here first, in plain words, where the mistake is
obvious.

### Problema
**Say what the article says, and say why it hurts.** Both halves, and the second is the one that goes
missing: a brief that names a situation without saying what is wrong with it leaves the reader asking
"and?" — which is the whole job undone.

- **Never trim the article to fit this line, and never leave the parts unnamed.** If the problem is
  the union of two or three things, list them one by one — each as a cost a person would actually
  feel — and then say why the combination is what hurts. "The problem is the sum" is not naming a
  sum.
- **Never state a pain in a shape that reads as a benefit.** "Everyone enters through a single door"
  sounds like convenience; if that is the problem, the line has to carry the cost that makes it one.
- Concrete over abstract: what the reader would actually feel — another console, another licence, a
  login at every start of shift.
- **When the article contradicts itself, say so** rather than quietly picking a side. An article that
  describes a workspace layer as locked to one provider, and then lists symptoms that only occur when
  it is spread across several, is describing two different situations. Name both, say which one the
  body of the article argues, and let the designer choose — the alternative costs her half an hour
  working out why nothing lines up.

### Palabras clave
**The words the article is about, ranked, covering it without repeating itself.** They are the seed
the designer starts from, so they carry the whole weight the retired devices used to.

**Ranked.** The title first, then the argument: the first word is what the article is most about —
what the title names, what the piece keeps returning to, what the rest rests on. A list in any other
order is not a ranking, it is four words.

**Covering.** Between them the words have to carry the article's different dimensions — **who and
where**, **what is at stake**, **the mechanism**, and anything the article insists on that none of
those reaches. A list that is all mechanism is blind, and so is a list that is all setting. Both have
happened here, in that order, one correcting the other too far.

**Without repeating itself.** Read the list back and ask what each word adds that the ones above it
did not. Four words pointing at the same world leave the designer with one idea, not four — and the
gap they leave is usually the thing the title puts up front.

- **Three or four is the usual size, and it is not a cap.** If the article genuinely has five or six
  things that matter and no two of them say the same thing, write them all, in order. Padding to
  four and trimming to four are the same mistake.
- **As general as the article allows, as specific as it requires.** If the article says "a nurse or
  physician", the word is the one that covers both — narrowing to one of them is a claim the article
  did not make. And a prop from a single sentence is not what the piece is about: the ward, not the
  med cart.
- **Concrete wherever the article gives you one.** A word that cannot be pictured is no use to a
  designer. Prefer the thing over the category: the screen, the badge, the record, the key — not
  "flexibility", "efficiency", "security".
- **Cover each word and ask whether the title still points at it.** If the whole list survives
  swapping the industry named in the title, the list is about the product rather than about this
  article.
- **They are not the SEO keyphrases.** Every article ships with a *Primary Keyphrase* and a list of
  secondary ones in its front matter. Those are written for a search engine and taking them is the
  easy, wrong answer: they name the market the article sells into, not what the article is about.
- **Not the title again.** If the words are the title's words in a different order, nothing was read.
- In Spanish, like the rest of the brief — keeping the article's own word where it is a real term of
  art, with the Spanish beside it if that helps.

## 4. Before delivering

Run this over the finished brief. Every line of it is a failure that has actually happened:

- The whole brief is **in Spanish**, not only its labels.
- **Sin tecnicismos** carries no jargon, no acronym and no product category, is an explanation rather
  than a scene, and its proposal would **not** be a true description of the situation today.
- **Problema** names its parts, rather than summarising them as "the sum".
- **Problema** says why it hurts, not only what happens.
- **Palabras clave** are ranked against the title first, and **no two of them say the same thing**.
- Between them they reach **who and where**, **what is at stake** and **the mechanism** — whichever
  of those the article has. Neither a list of pure mechanism nor a list of pure setting passes.
- No word is narrower than the article itself, none is the SEO keyphrase, and the set is not the
  title rearranged.
- Read back as a set: **this brief could not be mistaken for one about a different industry.**
- Nothing in the brief proposes composition, layout, lighting, palette or illustration style. Those
  are the designer's decisions, and a suggestion there is noise she has to argue with.
